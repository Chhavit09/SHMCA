            This run

            What it is

            Earth’s orbit is filling with debris. Orbital simulates satellites that look after themselves: they find nearby debris, judge the risk, burn fuel to dodge, fix software faults on their own, and call for repair when hardware fails.

            The simulation is the team’s C++ program orbital.cpp. This website runs a JavaScript copy of it in your browser, with the same tick order, gravity, chaos engine, threat scan and decisions, and shows the run in 3D. Runs start from real CelesTrak satellite and debris data or from satellites you type in, and are labelled “Browser simulation”.

            Replaying the files orbital.cpp writes is switched off for now.

            Ways to start a run

            1. Real satellites and debris
            New run → Real data. Two loaders, Satellite files and Debris files, each take several CelesTrak GP CSV files at once (button or drop). Every object in the files is used; a satellite listed in two files counts once. Station modules and docked spacecraft that CelesTrak lists separately with the station’s own orbit (such as POISK on the ISS) are joined to their station, because objects at the same spot would otherwise raise a satellite collision alert every second. All orbits start together at the newest epoch in the files. No debris is invented: the chaos engine still causes faults (solar flares, bugs, sensor faults) but adds no debris. Files dropped on the page are sorted automatically: names with DEB or R/B go to Debris.
            2. Your own satellites
            New run → Your own satellites. Type satellites (name, type and, in kilometre mode, altitude, inclination, RAAN and phase) in C++ units or kilometres. The Debris generator sets how much invented debris starts near each satellite and on its own orbits; the chaos engine adds more during the run. This is the only kind of run with invented debris.

            Get CelesTrak files with tools/get_celestrak_data.bat (Windows) or tools/get_celestrak_data.sh (macOS/Linux), which download three satellite groups (stations, weather, Iridium NEXT) and three debris clouds (Cosmos 2251, Iridium 33, Fengyun-1C).

            One tick inside orbital.cpp

            Every tick ( simulated second) the program runs these steps, in this order:

            1. Chaos enginePicks a random satellite. It may suffer a solar flare (6% chance, software −15 to −44%), battery drain (7%, −3 to −10%), thruster burnout (2%), a program bug (5%, software −35%) or a sensor fault (3%). With a 25% chance a new debris object appears near it.
            2. Debris movesGravity pulls each debris object towards Earth, then it moves one step.
            3. AVL tree builtEvery debris object is filed under its 3D sector (cubes of ). Red zones declared earlier are marked again.
            4. Satellites updateA satellite under repair counts down its repair time. Every other satellite moves, uses battery and fuel, and gets a health check from the flight computer.
            5. Telemetry writtenThe position (and health) of every object goes into telemetry.csv.
            6. Threat scanFor each working satellite: is its own sector a red zone flagged by another satellite? Then the AVL tree is searched for the 27 sectors around it. A sector holding 2 or more debris is declared a red zone. Any debris or satellite closer than the watch distance () becomes a threat in the min-heap.
            7. DecisionsThe min-heap hands over the nearest threat first. WARNING: logged only. DANGER or CRITICAL: collision alert; if the thruster works and 2.5 kg of fuel is left, an evasive burn pushes the satellite away; otherwise it is sent for repair.
            8. Log and statusEvery event is written to blackbox.txt; a status table is printed every 5 ticks. After the last tick you can search for a satellite by name.

            The browser simulator

            Both kinds of run are simulated by a JavaScript copy of orbital.cpp: the same tick order, gravity, chaos engine, thresholds, red zones and nearest-first threat order. The differences:

            Closest approach
            A crossing debris moves about 10 km relative to a satellite every second, so orbital.cpp, which checks distances once per tick, misses most close passes between two checks. With Check closest approach between ticks on (New run → Advanced, on by default), each tick works out from the relative position r and velocity v how close every nearby object will get during the next tick, |r + v·t*| with t* = −(r·v)/|v|² limited to one tick, and warnings, alerts and evasive burns use that distance. The alert therefore comes before the closest point. Turn it off to match orbital.cpp exactly.
            Neighbouring satellites
            Satellites are bucketed in a grid instead of comparing every pair as the C++ does; the result is the same, only faster with hundreds of satellites.
            Run size
            Telemetry holds one row per object per written tick, so 180 satellites and 4,000 debris over 600 written frames is about 2.5 million rows. One browser tab holds about 1.5 million comfortably and a run may take up to 2 minutes; the form estimates both as you type. A run over the limit shows a Fix button that writes telemetry less often (the run itself is unchanged; every tick is still simulated and checked) or, if the run is too slow, uses fewer ticks.
            Thresholds
            At 10 km, close passes with debris are genuinely rare. Real operators screen with zones of tens of kilometres; set Watch and Warning to 50, Danger 20, Critical 5 and Sector 50 under Advanced to see debris avoidance often.

            From simulation to screen (this website)

            1. SimulateThe browser copy of orbital.cpp runs every tick and writes, in memory, the same three outputs: telemetry.csv, blackbox.txt and run_info.txt.
            2. Settingsrun_info.txt gives the units, Earth radius and thresholds of the run.
            3. ParseTelemetry columns are read by name; each event line of the log becomes an event.
            4. IndexRows are grouped into frames, one per written tick.
            5. DeriveOnce: nearest distances, risk, repair periods, red-zone cubes, counters.
            6. DrawAbout 60 times a second: positions between written ticks are blended, rotated, projected and drawn far to near.

            Physics

            Classic mode
            Arbitrary units, Earth radius 4000. Gravity follows the C++ rule acceleration = 6.5² ÷ r, which keeps a circular speed of 6.5 units per tick. Satellites start 4800–5200 units out on nearly flat orbits.
            Real-data mode
            Kilometres and seconds. Newtonian gravity GM ÷ r² with GM = 398,600 km³/s²: a satellite at 400 km circles Earth in about 92 minutes at 7.7 km/s.
            Real orbits
            A CelesTrak file gives each object’s orbital elements at its epoch. They are turned into a position and velocity at the common start time with Kepler’s equation and the slow drift caused by the Earth’s flattening (J2). This approximates SGP4, the model CelesTrak’s elements are made for: expect a few kilometres of difference at the start, growing with time.
            Low and high orbits
            Most satellites and all debris clouds are in low Earth orbit, 400–1,000 km up, circling in about 90–105 minutes. Geostationary weather satellites (GOES, Meteosat, Himawari, Elektro, Fengyun-2, INSAT) are 35,786 km up, 6.6 Earth radii from the centre, and take a day per orbit, so they look far away and move only about 25° in a 100-minute run. Their dotted paths are short arcs for that reason.
            Moving objects
            Each tick: velocity += gravity × dt, then position += velocity × dt (semi-implicit Euler).
            Earth image
            In real-data runs the Earth turns once per sidereal day (23 h 56 min), starting from the run’s date, so continents sit roughly under the orbits.

            Risk levels and red zones

            Distance to the threatRiskWhat the satellite does
            or moreSafeNothing
            to WarningLogs a WARNING
            to DangerCollision alert, evasive burn (2.5 kg)
            under CriticalCollision alert, evasive burn (2.5 kg)

            Only objects inside the watch distance () are checked. In orbital.cpp the distance is the one at the tick; in browser runs with closest approach on, it is the closest the two will get during the next tick. A burn changes velocity by 0.08 directly away from the threat. With no thruster or not enough fuel, the satellite is dispatched for repair instead.

            Red zones: when a satellite finds 2 or more debris in one sector near it, it declares that sector a red zone for everyone. If another satellite later enters it, it logs a RED ZONE ALERT and, if it can, makes a diversion burn using 1.5 kg of fuel. Red zones are never cleared.

            Health and repair

            Starting state
            Battery 100%, software 100%, fuel 50 kg. Each tick uses 0.02% battery and 0.01 kg fuel.
            Health check
            A sensor or thruster fault sends the satellite for repair (DISPATCH). Otherwise it runs its own diagnostics.
            Communication satellites
            Signal falls 0.05% per tick and is re-tuned below 40%. Software under 70% is auto-patched back to 100%.
            Imaging satellites
            Calibration falls 0.05% per tick (0.5% when battery is under 30%) and is recalibrated below 50%. Software under 75% is auto-patched.
            Repair
            Takes the number of ticks entered at the start. Afterwards thruster and sensors work, software and battery are 100%, and 25 kg of fuel is added (up to 50 kg). In the C++ a satellite does not move while under repair.

            Limitations

            • Distances and risk on screen are worked out only at written ticks; between them positions are blended in a straight line, so a close pass between written ticks shows in the events but may not be drawn.
            • orbital.cpp checks distances only at whole ticks, so fast debris (about 10 km/s relative) can pass a satellite between two checks unseen. Browser runs can instead use each pair’s closest approach during the next tick (New run → Advanced); orbital.cpp does not.
            • Red zones are fixed boxes in space and are never cleared. At orbital speed debris leaves a small box within seconds, so many zones pile up.
            • The C++ thresholds are fixed at 15 / 10 / 5 and it burns fuel on every tick a threat lasts.
            • The Earth picture is a generated illustration with hand-traced coastlines, not satellite imagery.
            • The frosted-glass panels have to blur the moving scene behind them every frame. Runs with more than 1,500 objects therefore use solid panels, and so does any run that plays below 40 frames a second.
            • Every satellite shows its name and dotted orbit path. Where names would overlap, the farther ones wait until you zoom in or turn the view. With more than 60 satellites only the selected one has a fading trail, and above 150 the roster shows one line per satellite.

            Drag to rotate, scroll or pinch to zoom, double-click to reset. Point at an object for its details; click to select it. At 1× a typical satellite takes about 2½ minutes per orbit (about 40 times real time in low orbit); 0.1× and 0.25× slow it down to watch single events, and the line under the clock shows how fast simulated time is running. Clicking an event pauses at that moment; ← and → then step through it frame by frame. Burn focus (on by default, key B): whenever playback reaches an evasive burn, the camera flies to that satellite, replays the moment in slow motion and shows the threat with its live distance, the path the satellite was on before the burn (dashed, continued from its motion just before the burn with the simulation’s gravity) and the path it actually took (solid). Clicking an evasive-burn event replays it the same way.

            Burn focus (on by default; B or the Burn focus button switches it): at every evasive burn the playback slows down, the camera turns to the satellite, and a card compares the path it was on with the path it took. Drag the card by its top line to move it, set its see-through level with the slider in that line, and double-click the line to put it back; the website remembers both.

            Privacy

            Everything happens on your own computer. CelesTrak CSV files you load are read by your browser and never uploaded; the only network request is for the two fonts (Inter and JetBrains Mono) from Google Fonts, and there are no analytics, cookies or accounts. The links to CelesTrak only open when you click them.

            The page remembers one thing in your browser’s local storage on this computer: the position and opacity you chose for the evasive-burn card. Clearing the site data in your browser removes it.

            Numbers marked with units come from the loaded run’s settings (the classic C++ values until a run is loaded).
